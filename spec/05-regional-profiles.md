# Regional Profiles

## Status

Profiles are **research-informed starting points, not authenticity presets**. Each spans internally diverse places and periods. Before production use or public historical claims, narrow the profile to a specific context and obtain expert review. Names are navigational groupings, not assertions that one visual language uniformly covered every territory or century.

## Selection and no-blending

A composition uses `universal` or exactly one profile ID:

- `andalusi-maghrebi`
- `mamluk`
- `ottoman`
- `persian-central-asian`
- `mughal`

Profile-specific palettes, motif proportions, line treatments, vegetal vocabularies, and material cues MUST NOT be mixed. A shared circle, square, polygon, or grid is part of the universal core; a profile’s treatment of those primitives is not.

If the product spans regions, use separate clearly labeled sections or records, each reset to the neutral core before entering another profile. Never average profiles into a pan-Islamic visual. Historical transmission and exchange may be represented only through a sourced, expert-reviewed narrative—not through casual visual fusion.

## Profile schema

Each implementation records:

- exact scope: place(s), period/polity, medium(s);
- source objects and scholarship;
- permitted structural cues;
- permitted expressive cues;
- excluded or sensitive material;
- profile token overrides;
- reviewer names/roles and date;
- confidence and unresolved questions.

Color descriptions are relational. They are not universal palettes and must be sampled only from rights-cleared or independently constructed references.

## Andalusi/Maghrebi

### Scope

Starting frame: al-Andalus and the Maghreb, with particular attention to medieval and early modern architectural surface traditions. “Andalusi” and “Maghrebi” should be separated when evidence supports a narrower label; Nasrid Granada, Marinid Morocco, and other contexts are not interchangeable.

### Structural cues

- clear modular subdivision and repeated polygonal networks;
- strong figure/ground separation;
- bounded bands and dado-like zoning rather than all-over interface coverage;
- crisp interlace with legible crossings;
- orthogonal or polygonal framing that can organize page regions.

### Expressive cues

- restrained reference to assembled mosaic logic through flat vector cells;
- limited high-contrast color fields contained by a neutral frame;
- profile-specific star/polygon constructions only when derived independently from documented sources.

### Avoid

- calling every star mosaic “zellij” without evidence of material, place, and technique;
- horseshoe arches as generic control shapes;
- “Moorish” as an umbrella style label;
- importing Ottoman saz foliage, Safavid carpet medallions, or Mughal naturalistic flowers;
- tracing Alhambra ornament or contemporary artisan work.

### Review questions

Is the reference Andalusi, Maghrebi, or a later revival? Is it carved stucco, wood, ceramic mosaic, textile, or another medium? Has colonial-era cataloguing shaped the label?

## Mamluk

### Scope

Starting frame: Egypt and Syria under the Mamluk sultanates (1250–1517), with medium-specific attention to carved/inlaid wood, stone, metalwork, glass, and manuscript arts. Later “Mamluk revival” design must be labeled separately.

### Structural cues

- articulated panel systems and strong geometric joinery logic;
- interlocking star-and-polygon fields translated as assembly, not surface print;
- hierarchy between large structural panels and fine inlaid detail;
- darker/light material contrast expressed through flat, accessible tones.

### Expressive cues

- sparse ivory-like highlight against wood-like neutral only as an abstract color relationship, never fake material;
- elongated framed panels or bands inspired by joinery cadence;
- monumental clarity with tightly controlled fine detail.

### Avoid

- copying inscriptions from minbars, metalwork, mosque lamps, or architecture;
- separating geometry from a sacred furnishing and presenting it as context-free;
- using mosque furniture as a decorative product metaphor;
- mixing Ottoman tile florals or Andalusi mosaic cues into the profile.

### Review questions

Was the source religious, courtly, domestic, or commercial? Does it contain Qur’anic, dedicatory, heraldic, or patronage inscriptions? Are ivory and collection-history issues documented?

## Ottoman

### Scope

Starting frame: Ottoman court and architectural arts, especially sixteenth- and seventeenth-century design associated with Istanbul and Iznik production. Ottoman arts extend far beyond tilework; this profile must be narrowed when used.

### Structural cues

- spacious fields with asymmetrically balanced but controlled compositions;
- clear relationship between a central or rising stem and surrounding forms;
- repeated modules that preserve continuity across tile-like boundaries;
- strong white/quiet field supporting selective color.

### Expressive cues

- profile-approved vegetal vocabulary may include stylized composite blossoms, rosettes, tulips, carnations, and saz-related leaves when directly supported by sources;
- cobalt-like blue, turquoise-like accents, and later red accents MAY inform a constrained palette after source review;
- geometric framing remains secondary to vegetal movement where the selected source warrants it.

### Avoid

- labeling all blue-and-white ceramics as Ottoman or Iznik;
- using floral motifs without distinguishing vegetal ornament from geometry;
- copying mosque tile panels or their inscriptions;
- importing Mughal vase-and-flower arrangements or Safavid medallion systems;
- assuming a single “Ottoman palette” across centuries and media.

### Review questions

What production center, date, technique, and architectural context are documented? Is a term such as `saz` or `hatâyî` supported by the catalog source?

## Persian/Central Asian

### Scope

Starting frame: arts of Iran and Central Asia across multiple dynastic and workshop contexts. This is the broadest profile and MUST be narrowed—for example, Seljuk, Ilkhanid, Timurid, Safavid, a particular city, or a specific medium—before fidelity claims.

### Structural cues

- medallion-and-field hierarchy where source medium supports it;
- decagonal/girih construction only with explicit source and scholarly caveat;
- layered geometric networks and controlled scale shifts;
- strong boundary systems that separate field, medallion, spandrel, and border roles.

### Expressive cues

- vegetal scrolls, blossoms, and cloud-band-like forms only when tied to a sourced period and medium;
- deep field color with precise lighter detail may be considered for focal views;
- self-similar visual relationships MAY inform digital zoom or progressive disclosure, but MUST NOT be described as historical quasiperiodicity without evidence.

### Avoid

- treating “Persian,” “Iranian,” “Timurid,” and “Central Asian” as synonyms;
- copying Ardabil Carpet inscriptions or using its shrine provenance as decorative cachet;
- presenting the disputed girih/quasicrystal thesis as settled fact;
- combining star-and-cross shrine tiles with unrelated carpet or manuscript systems;
- importing Mughal pietra dura or Ottoman floral conventions without a sourced historical study.

### Review questions

Which polity, city, workshop, and medium? Is the object sacred, funerary, courtly, or commercial? Are inscriptions present? Does a museum record use outdated geography or attribution?

## Mughal

### Scope

Starting frame: Mughal South Asia, approximately sixteenth through eighteenth centuries, narrowed by reign, place, workshop, and medium. Mughal design reflects many exchanges and local practices; “Mughal” is not shorthand for all South Asian Muslim art.

### Structural cues

- lucid bilateral or radial organization with generous negative space;
- framed panels, repeated bays, and disciplined border/field relationships;
- geometry used to establish architectural clarity before ornament;
- fine detail concentrated at focal points rather than spread uniformly.

### Expressive cues

- carefully observed or stylized flowering plants and vase-and-flower arrangements when a documented source supports them;
- profile-specific inlay-like color segmentation may be abstracted without simulating stone;
- balanced garden-like ordering may inform content grouping, but avoid literal paradise symbolism unless the source and context warrant it.

### Avoid

- making onion-shaped arches, domes, or the Taj Mahal universal Mughal symbols;
- conflating Mughal, Rajput, Deccani, Sultanate, Kashmiri, or modern South Asian traditions;
- copying funerary or mosque decoration;
- importing Ottoman saz leaves or Persian carpet medallions merely because historical exchange occurred;
- calling any floral tile “Mughal.”

### Review questions

What reign, place, material, and function? Is the floral vocabulary naturalistic, stylized, imported, local, or hybrid according to scholarship? Does the source contain sacred or funerary associations?

## Cross-profile invariant tokens

Profiles MAY tune:

- non-semantic palette roles;
- ornamental line width within accessibility limits;
- one approved motif family;
- corner/frame treatment;
- ornament density downward;
- motion path character within the motion contract.

Profiles MUST NOT tune:

- semantic colors or status meaning;
- type size, text contrast, or reading order;
- Arabic shaping or language behavior;
- control semantics, target size, focus indication, or keyboard behavior;
- accessibility labels;
- sacred-content rules;
- provenance requirements.

## Approval statement

Every profile artifact carries one of:

- `research-draft`;
- `expert-review-pending`;
- `expert-reviewed` with scope and date;
- `community-reviewed` with scope and date;
- `deprecated`.

No artifact is labeled `authentic`. Review validates a bounded use at a point in time; it does not certify an entire culture.
